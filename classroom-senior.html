Testing a climate claim with real data

All versions

The claim: warming seas are giving bull sharks more time and more coastline, and bites have followed. Your job is to test it like a scientist, using the data from 19 Degrees (Researchers tab, then Download the data and images).

Bull shark bites below 19°C: 0 of 170. Coastal share: 4% (1900–59) to 68% (since 2015). WA: 10 since 2020 against 9 in 1900–2019.

Tasks

  1. Rate, not count. WA had 9 bull shark bites in 120 years and 10 in 6.5 years. Work out both yearly rates and how many times larger the recent rate is. Why compare rates rather than totals?
  2. Chance or change? The page reports p = 0.002 for the WA bull shark share of all shark bites. In one sentence, explain what that p-value means, and one thing it does not tell you.
  3. Other explanations. List three things besides warming that could explain bites moving into coastal waters (see "Could something else explain it?" in the page's Researchers tab). For each, describe evidence that would rule it in or out.
  4. Design a test. Pick one of the fifteen testable claims (C1–C15) in the page's Researchers tab. State the claim, the data you would need, who holds it, what result would support it and what would prove it wrong.
  5. Communicate. Write a 40-word social post that is accurate, not scary, and includes one number and one safety action.

Teacher notes

Ages 16 and up (Australia Years 10–12, US Grades 10–12, UK Years 11–13) · one 60–90 minute lesson, or two lessons with the data · individual or small groups

Learning intentions

  • Claims about climate and wildlife can be tested with real data.
  • Rates, p-values and alternative explanations all matter when judging evidence.
  • A good test says in advance what result would prove the claim wrong.

Success criteria

  • I can calculate and compare rates over different time periods.
  • I can explain what a p-value does and does not tell us.
  • I can evaluate alternative explanations and design a falsifiable test.

Big ideas

Testing a claim with real data. Rates versus counts, statistical significance and its limits, correlation versus cause, and what would prove a claim wrong.

Lesson sequence

PhaseTimeWhat happens
Engage10 minPresent the claim. Ask students to predict what evidence would convince them, and what would not.
Explore20 minTasks 1–2: rates and the p-value.
Explain10 minClass discussion: what does a p-value not tell us? Correlation versus cause.
Elaborate25 minTasks 3–4: alternative explanations and designing a test from the Researchers tab (C1–C15).
Evaluate10 minTask 5, then peer review of the test designs against the rubric.

Materials

Printed sheet; calculators. For two lessons: computers with the downloadable CSVs or the starter notebook (Python) from the Researchers tab.

Differentiation

Support: Model task 1 together; provide a worked example of interpreting a p-value.
Extension: Run the starter notebook, change the threshold from 19°C to 18.5°C and 19.5°C, and report how sensitive the rule is. Strong designs can be sent to 19 Degrees as a contribution.

Assessment

Rubric for task 4 (design a test). Excellent: falsifiable claim, appropriate data and holder named, a clear result that would disprove it, and a confounder addressed. Sound: testable claim and data named, disproof stated loosely. Developing: claim restated without a clear test.

Wellbeing

This topic involves injuries and deaths. Some students may know someone affected by a shark bite or a drowning. Keep the focus on patterns and staying safe, avoid graphic images and news footage, let students step out if they need to, and follow your school's wellbeing procedures. Sharks hold cultural significance for many Indigenous peoples, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander, Māori, Native Hawaiian and other Pacific peoples; if you explore this, use resources developed with or approved by local communities.

Answer guide. 1: 9/120 ≈ 0.07 a year against 10/6.5 ≈ 1.54 a year, about 21 times higher; rates allow for periods of different length. 2: if nothing had really changed, a difference this big would turn up by chance only about 2 times in 1,000; it does not say why the change happened or how big it matters. 3: identification (early river bites were labeled bull sharks partly by place), beach nets since the 1930s, rain and runoff plumes, more people surfing and spearfishing; test with confirmed-species bites, net locations, rainfall and river-mouth distance. 4: any claim with a measurable, falsifiable outcome. 5: for example, "Bull sharks never bite in water below 19°C, and that water now lasts 3 months longer off Sydney. Stay out of murky water after heavy rain and swim between the flags."
