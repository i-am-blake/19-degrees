Five facts for your project

    Questions nobody can answer yet

    A question becomes a claim once you can say what data would answer it. These four are still open. Each one lists the data a test would need.

    Q1SeasonNot yet tested

    What happens when sharks stop leaving?

    DetailsHide

    Bull sharks are already staying longer off Sydney, and young bull sharks are staying longer in warming US nursery waters. What that means for the places they used to leave, or now reach later, has barely been studied. A longer stay also means more months when people and sharks share the water. Whether they're leaving later is now claim C16.

    Lubitz et al. 2025; Matich et al. 2024

    Published evidence
    One or two studies, or one region
    To test it you would need
    Records of when sharks arrive and leave each year, from listening stations that stayed in the water.
    Became a claim
    Part of it: whether bull sharks leave Sydney later is claim C16.
    Q2SizeNot yet tested

    Will big sharks lose out to small ones?

    DetailsHide

    Some small bottom-dwelling sharks, like the epaulette shark, can survive warm tide pools with very little oxygen, so some scientists expect them to do better than big sharks that need a lot of food. But even epaulette shark embryos struggled near 31°C (88°F), the summer temperature expected on the Great Barrier Reef by 2100. They developed faster, hatched smaller and had to start feeding almost right away. Being small and tough is no guarantee.

    Wheeler et al. 2021

    Published evidence
    Early, small or disputed
    To test it you would need
    Counts of small and large sharks from the same places over many years, with the effort it took to find them.
    Q3GrowthNot yet tested

    Are warmer seas speeding up sharks' lives?

    DetailsHide

    Most sharks are cold-blooded, and even the few that keep parts of their body warm, like white sharks, run faster in warmer water. Embryos develop faster and burn through their energy reserves sooner, so young sharks need to find food earlier. Whether that means faster growth, shorter lives or more pressure on prey in the wild hasn't been measured.

    Wheeler et al. 2021

    Published evidence
    One or two studies, or one region
    To test it you would need
    Growth rings from shark backbones, from sharks caught decades apart.
    Q4SensesNot yet tested

    Does more acidic water dull a shark's sense of smell?

    DetailsHide

    In one lab study, smooth dogfish kept in water with the carbon dioxide levels expected by 2100 spent under 15% of their time tracking a food scent, compared with over 60% in normal seawater. But it was one small study, larger attempts to repeat similar results in reef fish failed, and the lead author was later found to have committed misconduct on other papers. Treat it as unproven.

    Dixson et al. 2015 · Clark et al. 2020 · Times Higher Education

    Published evidence
    Early, small or disputed
    To test it you would need
    The same experiment with other species, and evidence from the sea, not only a lab tank.

    Questions you could investigate

    1. How warm is the sea at the beach nearest you today? Is it above or below 19°C? (See "Right now" in the story.)
    2. Which months would a bull shark be able to visit Sydney in 1900, and which now?
    3. Why might bites move out of rivers and onto beaches? List three possible reasons and how you could check each.
    4. Why are there fewer oceanic sharks and rays than in 1970, and why does that matter?
    5. Tiger sharks eat sea turtles, and turtles are turning up further south. Could tracking turtles help predict when tiger sharks arrive? What data would you need?

    Key terms

    Sea surface temperature
    How warm the top few meters of the sea are.
    The 19°C line
    How far south the sea stays at 19°C (66°F) or warmer. No bull shark bite in Australia since 1900 happened in a month when the sea was cooler.
    Estuary
    Where a river meets the sea, so the water is part fresh, part salty.
    Range and season
    Range is where an animal can live; season is the part of the year it is there.
    Marine heatwave
    Days when the sea is much warmer than usual for that place and time of year.

    Stay safe: about 50 people drown on Australia's coast for every shark death. Swim between the red and yellow flags.
