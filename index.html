Australia, 1900 to today

170 bull shark bites. Not one below 19°C.

Sydney's sea now stays above 19°C (66°F) nine months a year. A century ago it was six.

    The bigger picture

    The ocean is one connected system. Every change ripples through it.

    The 19°C line moving off Sydney is one local sign of a global change. The ocean soaks up most of the heat trapped by greenhouse gases and much of the carbon dioxide, and the seas around Australia, especially the Tasman Sea, are warming faster than most. Warmer, more acidic water changes where plankton, fish, turtles and whales live, and the sharks that eat them follow.

    The warm current+350 kmThe East Australian Current now reaches about 350 km further south than 60 years ago, and the sea off eastern Tasmania is warming up to four times faster than the global average (Ridgway 2007, via Redmap). It drove the record 2015–16 Tasman Sea heatwave, 251 days long; climate change made a heatwave that long at least 330 times more likely (Oliver et al. 2017).
    Australia's seas+1.08°Csince 1900. The Tasman Sea, off Sydney and Eden, is warming at twice the global rate as the East Australian Current pushes further south (CSIRO and Bureau of Meteorology, 2024).
    Where the heat goes90%+of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases ends up in the ocean. 2025 set a new record for ocean heat, the ninth year running (Eos, 2026).
    Marine heatwaves+54%more heatwave days a year worldwide in 1987–2016 than in 1925–1954; heatwaves also became 34% more frequent and 17% longer (Oliver et al. 2018).
    Ocean chemistry~30%more acidic than before the industrial age (pH about 8.2 down to 8.1), because the ocean absorbs about a quarter of the carbon dioxide we emit (European Environment Agency).
    Marine life on the move+72 kma decade: how fast ocean species are shifting toward the poles on average, about 12 times faster than species on land, from 1,735 recorded changes worldwide (Poloczanska et al. 2013).

    In short: a change in one part of the ocean moves the whole food web. Warmer water reaching further south and staying longer means prey, predators and people now share more of the coast for more of the year.

    Not just Australia

    Other oceans show the same pattern, measured in different ways.

    If warming is moving sharks here, it should be happening elsewhere too. It is. These studies used tags, satellite tracking and fish surveys, not bite records, so they are independent evidence.

    What draws them in

    Sharks follow food and water conditions, not just temperature.

    Warm water opens the door. Rain, murky water and moving prey decide where, inside that window, sharks and people meet.

    After heavy rain

    The fastest-changing risk: days, not decades.

    1 to 4 daysBull shark Sydney Harbour

    Ten years of tracking 177 bull sharks and fish together found heavy rain moved all of them: males upstream within about a day, females within about four, and sharks spreading along the coast after big rain. Strong Niella et al. 2022

    4 bites in 3 daysBull shark Sydney, January 2026

    After 126.8 mm of rain on 18 January 2026, four people were bitten or had boards bitten, one fatally in Sydney Harbour. The shark-incident database lists all four as bull sharks. One event Shark-Incident Database · Netweather 2026

    Prey moving south

    The slower shift: what sharks hunt is changing along the coast.

    Kelp gone by 2009Reef fish Coffs Harbour

    As the water warmed, tropical fish arrivals rose steadily and some local temperate species declined. From video surveys, 2002–2018. Strong Smith, Vergés et al. 2021

    Turtles southTiger shark NSW

    Tiger sharks eat sea turtles, and turtles are turning up further south. Researchers expect prey like this to shape where tiger sharks go as much as temperature does. Expert view ABC 2026

    Can we see prey moving with the sharks in this page's own data? Not yet. The shark-incident database records the month but not the day of each bite, so it can't be matched to daily rain, and there is no open long-term record of baitfish along this coast to compare with. The tracking studies above are the best evidence. Data that could close the gap: IMOS animal tracking (sharks and fish on the same receivers) and Redmap (sightings of species outside their usual range).

    Could something else explain it?

    We checked the obvious alternatives.

    • It isn't more sharks. Oceanic sharks and rays are down about 71% worldwide since 1970, and big sharks in Queensland's nets are down 74–92%.
    • It isn't only more swimmers. Australia's population has grown more than sixfold since 1900, which helps explain more bites overall. It doesn't explain why bites have moved south, later into the year and into coastal waters.
    • It isn't one hot spell or El Niño. Unusually hot months don't bring more bites, and El Niño shows no effect on the east coast. What matters is a warm season that lasts.

    Staying safe

    What actually reduces bites, and why bull sharks are the hard case

    Most shark safety tools were designed for white sharks in clear water. Bull sharks bite in murky water, close to shore, and now later in the year.

    First, perspective

    For every shark death on Australia's coast, about 49 people drown.

    July 2015 to June 2025. Each dot is one life.

    1,277 coastal drownings26 fatal shark bites

    Swim between the red and yellow flags, and pick your time. Lifesavers watch for rips there, the bigger killer. After heavy rain, stay out of harbors and rivers for 72 hours and beaches for 24, and check the SharkSmart app.

    Shown to help

    Freedom+ SurfTested on 3 species

    An electric device for surfboards. Cut bites by 54% across white, tiger and bull sharks in a 2024 test. Against bull sharks alone it cut bait-taking by 42%, but they grew used to it over three weeks. Clarke et al. 2024 · Gauthier et al. 2020

    Rpela V2Tested, mixed

    A surfboard electric device. Cut white shark bites by about two thirds in 2021 (the first version showed no effect in 2018), but against bull sharks cut bait-taking by only 16%. Blount et al. 2021 · Gauthier et al. 2020

    Freedom7, for diversTested, mixed

    Cut tiger shark bites by 69% but made no difference for white sharks in the same 2024 study. In 2016 it kept white sharks about 1.3 m away on first approach. Clarke et al. 2024 · Kempster et al. 2016

    Listening stations and alertsIn use

    37 real-time stations, at least one in every NSW coastal council area and more being added in Sydney Harbour, detect tagged white, bull and tiger sharks and alert the SharkSmart app. They only hear sharks that have been tagged. NSW DPI

    Stopping bleeding fastTested first aid

    Most shark-bite deaths come from blood loss, often within minutes. In a 2021 trial with healthy volunteers, pressing a fist hard into the groin crease cut blood flow to the leg by about 90%, twice as well as a surfboard leg-rope tourniquet; the technique is not yet in Australian Resuscitation Council guidelines. About 250 community bleeding kits with tourniquets now sit at beaches in NSW and Western Australia (mid-2026), and one was used in June 2026 after a white shark bite at Coogee; the woman survived. Leashes with built-in tourniquets are sold; the tourniquet in one of them passed a 2020 peer-reviewed self-application test, but no study has tested the leash in the surf. In an emergency, call your local emergency number first (000 in Australia, 911 in North America, 112 across Europe and on most mobile phones worldwide). This is research, not first-aid instruction: learn bleeding control from an accredited first-aid course. Taylor et al. 2021 · Canberra Health Services 2022 · Surfer Today on Community Shark Bite Kits, 2026

    Helps, with limits

    Shark netsOfficial report

    In the 2025–26 season, nets at 51 beaches from Newcastle to Wollongong caught 210 animals. Only 36 were target sharks (23 white, 9 tiger, 4 bull); two thirds of all animals caught died, and 48 were threatened species such as grey nurse sharks, turtles and a humpback whale. Since 2024 the nets come out on 31 March, a month earlier, to protect turtles.

    NSW Shark Meshing Program 2025–26 annual report · 2024–25 report · SharkSmart NSW

    Smart drumlinesIn use

    Alert a crew when a shark takes the bait so it can be tagged and released offshore. NSW now runs 305 year-round along its coast. In the first south coast trial, all 11 sharks caught were released alive, but none were bull sharks. NSW Government, January 2026 · NSW DPI 2019

    DronesIn use, field tests

    By late January 2026, NSW lifesaver drones had flown 29,431 flights that season, spotted 461 sharks and supported 170 beach evacuations or alarms. In Queensland’s 2020–24 trial at 10 beaches, drones recorded 676 shark sightings while nets and drumlines at the same beaches caught 284, despite flying only about 3% of the time the nets were set; murky water cut how many sharks drones could see. Their limit is murky water: in tests, almost no shark-shaped targets were seen 2 m down when visibility was under 1.5 m. NSW Government 2026 · Mitchell et al. 2025, Queensland drone trial report · Butcher et al. 2019

    Bite-resistant wetsuitsTested, white and tiger

    Four bite-resistant materials all cut severe tearing from white and tiger shark bites compared with ordinary 3 mm neoprene, the kind of wound that causes heavy bleeding, with little difference between them. They don’t stop a bite or crushing injuries, haven't been tested with bull sharks, and no study has yet looked at real incidents. Clarke et al. 2025, Wildlife Research

    Sonar buoysTrial

    Correctly identified white sharks 45% of the time within 46 m. A useful extra layer, but it misses more than half. NSW Shark Management Strategy

    Boat-sized electric fieldTested, white sharks

    Ocean Guardian BOAT01 is designed to protect an area about 8 by 6 m. In a 2022 test with 43 white sharks it cut bait-taking by up to 48%, but only worked within about 3 m. Riley et al. 2022

    No effect found, or not shown

    Patterned "shark camouflage" wetsuitsNot shown

    Striped or patterned wetsuits are sold to break up a surfer's outline. The only trial we found, with white sharks in 2016, was promoted by the maker and has not been peer reviewed. Sharks are probably colour-blind and see mostly outline, which is why researchers moved to lights: in 2024, light stripes on seal-shaped decoys deterred white sharks, but they haven't been tested on surfers yet. Business News 2016 (sponsored) · Ryan et al. 2021

    Magnetic bands and leashesNo effect found

    SharkBanz bracelet and leash: no measurable difference to how often white sharks took the bait. Huveneers et al. 2018

    Shark-repellent surf waxNo effect found

    Chillax Wax: no measurable difference with white sharks. Huveneers et al. 2018

    Two more surfboard electric devicesNo effect found

    E-Shark Force and NoShark did not reduce bait-taking by bull sharks. With E-Shark Force, sharks touched or took the bait 97% of the time, the same as with no device. Gauthier et al. 2020

    Electric ankle bandLittle effect

    The Electronic Shark Defense System did not reduce how many white sharks came to the bait, and only worked within about 15 cm. Egeberg et al. 2019

    Barriers in the surfTrial stopped

    Two NSW north coast trials stopped because neither barrier could be installed effectively and safely in ocean surf. NSW Shark Management Strategy

    A 2025 review of 67 studies found electric devices the most effective personal option, working mainly within about 2 m, and well-maintained barriers the only measure likely to fully prevent contact. It also found few studies measured actual bites, and there is no standard way to test. No deterrent stops every bite and there is no Australian standard for them, so look for a published, independent test with the shark species found where you swim. 19 Degrees summarises published research; it does not recommend or endorse any product and is not safety, medical or legal advice. Always follow lifesavers, local authorities and beach signs. See all 26 products we checked. Riley, Bradshaw and Huveneers 2025, Marine Policy

    Two things the data can't answer yet

    White sharkOur data

    Are more people in the water when sharks are?

    Open

    Hot days send more people to the beach, and marine heatwaves can push fish and sharks inshore. Both could put more people and sharks in the same shallow water at once, but no study has measured the two together for Australia. It matters, because bites depend on people as much as on sharks.

    What that means for the years ahead

    1. Expect bull sharks off more of the coast, for more of the year. The warm season is already months longer, and bites now come in autumn and winter, not just summer.
    2. Watch the rain. Bull shark risk rises in murky water after heavy rain: wait 72 hours in harbors and rivers and 24 hours at ocean beaches.
    3. The water is still the bigger danger. About 49 people drown on Australia's coast for every shark death. Swim between the red and yellow flags.

      Sea surface temperature
      How warm the top few meters of the sea are.

      How far south the sea stays at 19°C (66°F) or warmer. No bull shark bite in Australia since 1900 happened in a month when the sea was cooler.
      Estuary
      Where a river meets the sea, so the water is part fresh, part salty.

      Range is where an animal can live; season is the part of the year it is there.

      C1RangeEarly result: holds in Queensland

      Bull shark catch rates in shark-control gear are rising at the southernmost beaches, not just bites.

      C11Bleeding productsOpen

      A tourniquet built into a surf leash stops blood flow to the leg as well as a standard medical tourniquet, when an untrained surfer applies it in under a minute.

      Already known
      A 2020 peer-reviewed study found the OMNA marine tourniquet, the device built into one leash, could be self-applied effectively in all 540 test applications by volunteers, including one-handed.       C13Beach readinessOpen

New places matter too: models suggest tiger sharks could reach Tasmania's east coast by 2030, and one was detected at Maria Island for the first time in 20 years of monitoring (Niella et al., reported by ABC 2026).
      Supports it if
      a map of readiness (kits, patrol days, ambulance response) shows gaps concentrated where this page's warm season is growing fastest, such as the NSW south coast.
      Rules it out if
      readiness is spread evenly, or best where warming is fastest.
      Data
      Kit locations, patrolled beaches and seasons, ambulance response times, this page's beach-by-beach warm months
      Who holds it
      Community Shark Bite Kits; Surf Life Saving (Beachsafe); state ambulance annual reports; 19 Degrees data
      Skills
      GIS, data joining · Mostly public; kit locations on request
      C14WetsuitsOpen

      Bite-resistant wetsuits reduce injuries from bull shark bites, not just white and tiger shark bites, and reduce severe bleeding in real incidents.

      Already known
      Two studies with white sharks (2019) and white and tiger sharks (2025) found bite-resistant fabrics cut punctures and severe tearing compared with neoprene. Neither tested bull sharks or measured injuries to people. Whitmarsh et al. 2019 · Clarke et al. 2025
      Products to test
      SharkStop, ActionTX-S, Aqua Armour and Brewster bite-resistant fabrics, against ordinary neoprene.
      Supports it if
      bait trials with bull sharks show the same drop in severe tearing as for white and tiger sharks, and real incidents involving these suits show less severe wounds.
      Rules it out if
      bull shark bites, which crush as much as cut, do as much damage through the material, or real wounds are no less severe.
      Data
      Material bite trials with bull sharks; incident reports noting what the person wore and the injury
      Who holds it
      Flinders University (Huveneers group); Taronga Conservation Society (incident details, on request); manufacturers' sales records
      Skills
      Field trials; injury classification · New fieldwork; incident details on request
      C15Wetsuits and boardsEarly result: works on decoys

      Lights that break up a surfer's outline from below, proven on seal-shaped decoys, also reduce white shark approaches to real surfboards, and work for bull and tiger sharks.

      Already known
      Partly tested. In a 2024 study off Mossel Bay, South Africa, LED lights brighter than the background on the underside of seal-shaped decoys deterred white sharks, and stripes across the direction of travel worked best. It used decoys, not surfboards or people, and only white sharks; the authors say more experiments are needed before it's used to protect people. Ryan et al. 2024, Current Biology
      Products to test
      Counter-illumination light prototypes; SAMS patterned wetsuits as a comparison.
      Supports it if
      boards fitted with light stripes are approached and bitten clearly less often than plain boards in blind, independent trials, including with bull and tiger sharks.
      Rules it out if
      approach rates on boards are the same, or sharks get used to the lights within a session.
      Data
      Board and decoy trials with white, bull and tiger sharks, recorded on video and scored blind
      Who holds it
      Macquarie University neurobiology lab (Ryan, Hart); Flinders University
      Skills
      Field trials, video analysis · New fieldwork; needs animal ethics approval and permits

      Open questions

      What scientists still don't know

      Some ideas about sharks and climate change are well tested, some rest on a single study, and some are still guesses. Each is marked with how strong the evidence is. Two more, white sharks moving north and people in the water, are in the story.

      Strongseveral studies agreeSomeone or two studies, or one regionWeakearly, small or disputedOpennot yet tested

      What happens when sharks stop leaving?

      Some

      Bull sharks already stay longer off Sydney, and pups stay longer in warmer US nurseries. What it means for the places they would normally leave, or arrive in later, has hardly been studied. A longer stay also means more months in which people and sharks share the water, which is the risk this page measures.

      Lubitz et al. 2025; Matich et al. 2024

      Will big sharks lose out to small ones?

      Weak

      Some small bottom-dwelling sharks, such as the epaulette shark, can survive very low oxygen and warm tide pools, so some expect them to fare better than big sharks that need lots of food. But even epaulette embryos struggled near 31°C, the summer temperature expected on the Great Barrier Reef by 2100: they developed faster, hatched smaller and had to feed almost immediately. Being small and tough is not a guarantee.

      Wheeler et al. 2021

      Are warm seas speeding up shark lives?

      Some

      Sharks are cold-blooded or partly warm-blooded, so warmer water speeds up their bodies. Embryos develop faster and use up their energy sooner, so young sharks need more food sooner. Whether that means faster growth, earlier death or more pressure on prey in the wild hasn't been measured.

      Wheeler et al. 2021

      Does acidic water dull a shark's sense of smell?

      Weak

      In one lab study, smooth dogfish kept in water with the carbon dioxide expected by 2100 spent under 15% of their time following a food scent, against over 60% for normal water. But it was one small study, larger efforts to repeat similar results in reef fish failed, and the lead author was later found to have committed misconduct on other papers. Treat this as unproven.

      Dixson et al. 2015 · Clark et al. 2020 · Times Higher Education

      Will sharks switch to new, tropical prey?

      Open

      Tropical fish are replacing kelp-forest fish on reefs off NSW. Whether local sharks change what they eat, or warm-water sharks moving south take over, hasn't been tested.

      Smith, Vergés et al. 2021

      Robustness checks

      Could something else explain it?

      The warming explanation has to survive the other big changes in the ocean and on the beach. Here are the six alternatives people raise most, what we checked, and the verdict.

      More sharks in the waterUnlikely

      Oceanic sharks and rays fell about 71% worldwide between 1970 and 2018, mainly from fishing, and catch rates of big sharks in Queensland's shark nets fell 74–92% over 50 years. Rising bites near some beaches aren't a sign of more sharks overall. Neither study counts bull sharks separately, so a local rise in bull sharks can't be ruled out; claim C1 tests that. Pacoureau et al. 2021 · Roff et al. 2018

      More people in the waterExplains part

      Australian Bureau of Statistics

      Bites blamed on the wrong sharkFindings hold

      Many bites are put down to a species from what a witness saw, which can be wrong. If bites were often blamed on the wrong shark, the patterns could be a mistake rather than a real change. So we re-ran the findings using only bites where the species was confirmed by bite analysis, a tooth, DNA, a photo or video. There are fewer bites, but the proportions barely change, so the pattern holds: Australian Shark-Incident Database

      Better reporting todayShares unaffected

      Far more bites are reported now than a century ago, which inflates counts. The main findings are shares, the share of bites in water below 19°C and the share in coastal waters, which don't depend on how many bites get reported. They would only shift if reporting improved more for beaches than for rivers, which these records can't check. This page's method

      El Niño and La NiñaNo effect on the east coast

      Niño 3.4 index from HadISST; Australian Shark-Incident Database

      One-off hot spellsNot the driver

      In early 2011 the "Ningaloo Niño" heatwave pushed the sea off Western Australia up to 5°C above normal, yet no bull shark bites were recorded there from 2000 to 2014. What matters is a warm season that lasts. CSIRO · this page's data

      Whales coming backNot for bull sharks

      Humpbacks off eastern Australia recovered from as few as 200–500 in 1962 to about 24,000 by 2015. Whale calves and carcasses feed tiger and white sharks along the migration route, which could bring them near the coast in winter and spring. It doesn't explain bull shark patterns, and hasn't been tested for white or tiger shark bites. Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority

      What this adds up to. Fewer sharks overall, more people, no help from El Niño on the east coast, and no effect of one-off hot spells, yet a longer warm season and bites moving south, later in the year and into coastal waters. Warming is the explanation that fits all of these together; it doesn't have to be the only cause to be the main one. Claim C1 now adds Queensland's shark-control catches to the picture.

      Why other studies find different thresholds. Mobile Bay's 22.5°C comes from a model of where young sharks were caught in a warm bay; this page's 19°C is a monthly average for the open sea offshore, which runs cooler than the harbors and rivers where bull sharks bite. Different places, life stages and measurements give different numbers. What every study finds is that there is a line, and it is moving poleward.

      What to send

      4. Your result, including if it didn't support the claim.

      What gets added

      1. Results others can reproduce from data they can access.
      2. Negative results, which matter as much as positive ones.
      3. Work that respects data licenses and privacy. Never contact people who were bitten or their families for this project.

      How you're credited

      1. Your name, and a link to your work, on the claim card.
      2. An entry in "What has changed" with the date.
      3. The claim's status updated: supported, not supported or mixed.

      Standards every result is held to

      Before you start

      1. Write down your hypothesis, data, method and what would prove it wrong before you look at the result. A public pre-registration (for example on OSF) is ideal.
      2. Get the approvals your work needs: human research ethics approval for records about people, such as coroner files, and animal ethics approval and permits for fieldwork with sharks.
      3. Check the data licence allows your use, and cite it.

      When you report

      1. Give the effect size with a 95% confidence interval, not just a p-value.
      2. Say how many tests you ran, and label anything you found by exploring rather than planned.
      3. Share code and data, or exactly how to get them, so someone else can reproduce the result.
      4. Disclose funding and any conflict of interest, including links to deterrent makers or advocacy groups.

      For teachers, students and scientists

      Words, methods and sources

      Words used on this page
      Sea surface temperature
      How warm the top few meters of the sea are. Here it is a monthly average for a 1° square of open sea, about 110 km across.
      The 19°C line
      How far south the sea stays at 19°C (66°F) or warmer. No bull shark bite in Australia since 1900 happened in a month when the sea was cooler.
      Estuary
      Where a river meets the sea, so the water is part fresh and part salty. Bull sharks can live in both.
      Marine heatwave
      A stretch of at least five days when the sea is much warmer than usual for that place and time of year.
      Ocean acidification
      The sea absorbs carbon dioxide from the air, which makes it slightly more acidic.
      Range and season
      Range is where a species can live; season is the part of the year it is there.
      Statistically significant
      Very unlikely to be a fluke of chance. A p-value of 0.002 means that if nothing had really changed, a result this big would turn up by luck only about 2 times in 1,000.
      Methods
      • Bites. Every bite in the Australian Shark-Incident Database since 1900 with a known month, place and species (white, tiger or bull).
      • Water temperature at each bite. That month's HadISST sea surface temperature for the 1° square of sea nearest the bite. It is an open-sea monthly average, so it says how warm the season was, not the exact water the person was in.
      • Seasons. For each beach, the months each year with an average of 19°C or more, from 1900 to the latest month available, counted in July-to-June seasons.
      • Bite zones. The middle 80% of each species' bites by latitude on the Queensland and NSW coast, in four eras.
      • Inshore bites. Site type of river or estuary/harbor in the database, or a place name containing harbor, river, creek, lake or estuary.
      • Western Australia test. A Poisson test of 2020s bull shark bites against the 1900–2019 yearly rate, and a one-sided Fisher exact test of the bull shark share of all WA shark bites, 2000–2019 against 2020 on.
      • El Niño and La Niña. Each month since 1950 labeled El Niño (index +0.5 or above), La Niña (−0.5 or below) or neutral; bull shark bites per month in each, with a two-sided binomial test of the La Niña share.
      • 2050 look-ahead. Each beach's 1980–2025 warming trend carried 30 years forward from its 2016–2025 average month. A simple extrapolation, not a climate model. The bite scenario holds bites per warm-water month at the 2015-on rate, with an 80% Poisson range.
      • Limits. Bites are rare, so small numbers swing a lot; reporting and species identification have improved over time; and bites depend on how many people are in the water, which isn't recorded.

      News and media

      The findings, the images, and how to report them accurately.

      Key findings

        Story angles

        It isn't more sharks

        Shark numbers worldwide have fallen sharply, yet bites are moving south, later into the year and into coastal waters. The story is where and when people and sharks now meet.

        Western Australia's bull shark jump

        More bull shark bites since 2020 than in the previous 120 years combined, very unlikely to be chance. Half were in the tropical north, half around Perth.

        Out of the rivers

        Bull shark bites used to happen in harbors and rivers. Most now happen in coastal waters, often after heavy rain.

        Sydney in 2050

        At the pace of warming since 1980, the sea off Sydney would be warm enough for bull sharks almost all year by 2050.

        Getting it right

        • Say "bites," not "attacks." Most bites are investigative or mistaken; researchers and Australian agencies use "bite" or "incident."
        • It is not "more sharks." Oceanic sharks and rays are down about 71% worldwide since 1970. What has changed is where and when people and sharks overlap.
        • 19°C is a monthly open-sea average, not a reading at the beach on the day. Treat it as a seasonal line, not a daily safety cutoff.
        • Link, not proof. Warming fits every pattern here, but identification, beach nets, rain and more people in the water also play a part. The page says so.
        • Expect the obvious questions. Isn't it just more sharks? No: sharks are down worldwide. More people? That explains more bites, not bites moving south and into coastal waters. Misidentified sharks? The findings hold with confirmed bites only. El Niño? No effect on the east coast. The full checks.
        • Put Australia in context. In 2025 there were 65 confirmed unprovoked shark bites worldwide, close to the 2020–2024 average of 61. Australia had 21, five of them fatal, and the United States 25 (International Shark Attack File).
        • Keep the perspective. About 49 people drown on Australia's coast for every shark death.

        Australian shark bites and sea temperature, 1900–2026. Bites: Australian Shark-Incident Database (Taronga Conservation Society and Flinders University; bites with a known month and place). Monthly sea temperature: HadISST (UK Met Office), via NOAA ERDDAP, for a 1° patch of open sea off each region or beach, which runs a little warmer than water at the beach. Cross-checked against NOAA ERSSTv5, which shows the same lengthening of Sydney's 19° season. Today's readings: Open-Meteo Marine. Drownings: coastal deaths estimated from Surf Life Saving Australia's National Coastal Safety Report 2025 ten-year averages. Bull shark tagging: Lubitz et al. 2025, Science of the Total Environment. Look ahead to 2050: each beach's 1980–2025 warming trend in the same sea temperature record, added to its 2016–2025 average month; a simple extrapolation, not a climate model. Australian seas have warmed 1.08°C since 1900, and the Tasman Sea is warming at twice the global rate (CSIRO and Bureau of Meteorology, State of the Climate 2024). Map: Natural Earth. Fewer people swim in cold water, but white sharks still bite there, so people are in the water when bull sharks are not. Many bull shark bites, especially before 2000, happened in harbors and rivers, where the water can be warmer than the open-sea patch it is measured against.
