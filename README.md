# 19 Degrees

### Know where the sharks are going, and what actually keeps people safe.

The ocean is warming, and the animals in it are moving. Marine species worldwide are shifting toward the poles by about **72 km a decade, roughly 12 times faster than life on land** ([Poloczanska et al. 2013](https://www.nature.com/articles/nclimate1958)). Sharks are part of that move. So are the millions of people who swim, surf and fish on the coasts where those sharks are arriving.

**Our mission is to protect sharks and people as the ocean warms: advancing shark and climate research worldwide, finding out which safety measures actually work, and building an open evidence base so coasts can prepare before the first bite, not after it.** The aim is to become the largest open research effort on sharks and a warming ocean, built by people all over the world.

**[See the live site →](https://19-degrees.org/)** Updated every week with new data, and every day with today's sea temperatures.

## Why this needs to exist

The problem isn't a lack of shark research. It's that the research is scattered. Bite records sit in one database, tracking data in another, sea temperatures somewhere else. Deterrent tests are buried in journals few people read, and every state and country judges its own safety programs on its own terms. No one has put it all in one place, measured the same way, open to everyone.

19 Degrees brings it together: one open harbor where bite records, ocean data, tracking studies and safety evidence come in, so scientists, agencies, lifesavers, educators and swimmers can all work from the same facts.

## It starts with one line in the water

Australia has one of the longest shark-bite records on Earth, so we started there. Every bull, tiger and white shark bite since 1900 is matched to that month's sea temperature. One number stood out:

> **170 bull shark bites. Not one in water below 19°C.**

Tagged sharks agree: of 20,105 days that 132 tagged bull sharks were detected off NSW, 99.5% fell in months when the sea was 19°C or warmer.

That line is moving. Off Sydney, water at 19°C or more now lasts about 45% longer each year than it did a century ago, and it reaches about 215 km further down the coast. The bites have followed: further south, later in the year, and out of estuaries, harbors and rivers into coastal waters, where 68% of bull shark bites since 2015 have happened, up from 4% before 1960.

None of this means more sharks. Oceanic sharks and rays are down about 71% worldwide since 1970 ([Pacoureau et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-03173-9)), and in Australia drowning on the coast takes about 49 lives for every one lost to a shark. It means **fewer sharks, closer encounters**, and a changing map of where to be ready.

## Does it actually work?

Beaches, governments and swimmers spend millions on shark safety: nets, drumlines, drones, electric deterrents, magnetic bands, bite-resistant wetsuits, barriers, apps, bleeding kits. Some are proven. Many have never been independently tested. A few have been tested and found to do nothing. People choose between them with little more than marketing to go on.

19 Degrees holds every shark-safety measure to the same standard: **a published, independent test, linked, with the result stated plainly, whatever it is.**

So far we've checked 26 products against the published evidence:

| What independent tests found | Products |
|---|---|
| Reduced bites | 1 |
| Reduced injury when a bite happens (bite-resistant fabrics) | 5 |
| Small or mixed effect | 4 |
| Partly tested | 6 |
| No effect found | 7 |
| Not peer reviewed | 2 |
| Trial stopped | 1 |

Beyond products, the site rates beach-level measures (nets, drumlines, drones, listening stations, barriers) and first aid the same way. Each is labelled "shown to help", "helps, with limits" or "no effect found, or not shown", with the study behind it.

**The goal:** a living, worldwide scorecard for shark safety that anyone can check. It covers:

- **Products:** every deterrent, wetsuit, leash and bleeding-control device, tested against the species that bite people most, especially bull and tiger sharks, where the evidence is thinnest.
- **Beach programs:** nets, drumlines, drones and alerts, judged by bites prevented and by the harm they cause to other wildlife.
- **Readiness:** whether bleeding kits, patrols and faster first aid save lives. Most shark-bite deaths come from blood loss, and nobody has yet measured what being prepared changes.

Six of the eighteen testable claims (C10–C15) are about safety: drones in murky water, tourniquet leashes, bleeding kits, beach readiness, bite-resistant wetsuits and counter-illumination lights. Researchers, makers and lifesaving organizations: if you have test results, positive or negative, we'll add them. If a product you make isn't listed, the fastest way onto the scorecard is an independent, published trial.

*Listing is not endorsement. Results summarize published independent tests as of October 2026, and products may have changed since they were tested.*

## Where this goes next

- **Every coast, measured the same way.** Tiger sharks, young white sharks and whale sharks are already shifting in other oceans. The method here is open so it can be rerun wherever long-term records exist: the United States, South Africa, Brazil, Réunion, the Pacific islands.
- **Beaches that see their warm season coming.** Which months, which species, how fast it's changing, for any beach, in plain language.
- **Coasts that are ready.** Bleeding-control kits, patrols and first aid placed where the warm water is going, not only where it has been. Most deaths from shark bites are from blood loss, and many are survivable when bleeding is stopped within minutes.
- **Science anyone can check.** Open data, open code, eighteen testable claims, and named credit for everyone who tests one.
- **Perspective, not fear.** Real numbers for swimmers, students, reporters and decision-makers, and a reminder that sharks are in trouble too.

## Fund the next stage

19 Degrees is independent and free. Funding goes to work that no single lab or agency is set up to do:

1. **More coastlines.** Rerun the method for the United States, South Africa and other coasts with long-term bite records.
2. **The beach-readiness map.** Where bleeding kits, patrols and ambulance times fall short of where the warm water is going (claim C13).
3. **Closing the safety evidence gap.** Independent trials of deterrents and bite-resistant wetsuits with bull and tiger sharks, run with research partners (claims C14 and C15).
4. **Keeping it free and current.** Weekly data updates, hosting and classroom materials.

Foundations, ocean and outdoor brands, and research institutions: [let's talk](https://19-degrees.org/#contact).

## Data sources

- **Shark bites:** [Australian Shark-Incident Database](https://zenodo.org/records/18752301) (Taronga Conservation Society Australia and Flinders University), CC BY 4.0
- **Sea temperature, 1900 to now:** [HadISST](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/hadobs/hadisst/) (UK Met Office), Open Government Licence, accessed through NOAA ERDDAP
- **El Niño and La Niña:** Niño 3.4 index, calculated from HadISST
- **Today's sea temperature:** Open-Meteo Marine
- **Shark-control catches (claim C1):** Queensland Shark Control Program open data
- **Coastal drownings:** Surf Life Saving Australia, National Coastal Safety Report 2025
- **Shark tracking:** [IMOS Animal Tracking](https://researchdata.edu.au/imos-animal-tracking-2007-ongoing/3961802) acoustic detections of bull, tiger and white sharks and green turtles (Integrated Marine Observing System), CC BY 4.0, checked monthly. Fetched by `.github/workflows/fetch-imos.yml`; analysed by `src/imos19.py`
- **Population:** Australian Bureau of Statistics
- **Safety evidence and other figures:** published studies, each linked on the site
