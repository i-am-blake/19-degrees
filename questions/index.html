01How many shark attacks are there a year worldwide?CountedAbout 60 confirmed unprovoked bites a year. In 2025 there were 65, and 9 were fatal.2025 figures; the 2026 count comes out early in 2027
  • Recent years: 69 in 2023, 47 in 2024 and 65 in 2025.
  • 2025: United States 25, Australia 21, the Bahamas 5, New Zealand 3, and one each in 11 other places, including South Africa, Canada and New Caledonia.
  • The 2020–2024 average was 61 bites and 8 deaths a year.
  • Counts differ a little by source: this one has 21 for Australia in 2025, the Australian database 22, because they classify some cases differently.
  • Each year's worldwide count comes out early the next year, so 2026 isn't counted yet.

03Are sharks endangered?CountedMany are. Bull sharks and great white sharks are listed as Vulnerable, and tiger sharks as Near Threatened.IUCN listings checked November 2026
  • IUCN Red List: bull shark Vulnerable (assessed 2021, population decreasing), great white shark Vulnerable, tiger shark Near Threatened.
  • In the Mediterranean, white sharks are listed as Critically Endangered (IUCN regional assessment, 2016).
  • Oceanic sharks and rays have declined by 71% since 1970, mainly because fishing pressure rose about 18-fold (Pacoureau and colleagues, Nature, 2021).
  • About a third of shark and ray species are threatened with extinction.

05Where do bull sharks live?Some evidenceIn warm coastal water and up rivers. In Australia, every one of 170 bull shark bites since 1900 happened where the sea was 19°C or warmer.Evidence as of 26 November 2026

03Why are there so many sharks in WA?Some evidenceRecorded bites in WA have risen every decade, and almost half are white sharks. Why is only partly known.Bite records to June 2026
  • Unprovoked bites in WA: 7 in the 1990s, 23 in the 2000s, 41 in the 2010s and 44 from 2020 to 2025. WA's share of Australia's bites rose from 19% in the 2000s to 33% since 2020.
  • Of 108 WA bites since 2000, 50 were by white sharks, 17 by wobbegongs and 8 by bull sharks; 19 were fatal.
  • What's missing: how many more people are in the water, and WA's tagged-shark detections, which we've asked the state for.

04When is shark season in Sydney?Some evidenceFor bull sharks, the water off Sydney is warm enough from about late September to early July. That's about seven weeks longer than in the 1980s.Sea temperatures 2016–2025
  • Daily sea temperatures, 0.25° cell off Sydney: 19°C or warmer from about 25 September to 3 July in a typical recent year (2016–2025), against 30 October to 15 June in 1982–1992.

05When is shark season in WA?Early evidenceThere isn't a clear one. WA's bites come all year; around Perth, white shark bites cluster in spring.Bite records to June 2026
  • Perth area since 2000: 35 unprovoked bites, 15 of them by white sharks, and 7 of those 15 in October or November.
  • Across WA, white shark bites peak in March and October (7 each since 2000), but the numbers are small.
  • The 19°C bull shark season doesn't fit WA well: its main species is the white shark.

02When is shark season in Florida, Hawaii, California, Maui, San Diego or Myrtle Beach?Partly answeredHawaii: incidents come all year, but October has the most, about twice an average month (32 of 174 on the state's list since 1995). Hawaii DLNR shark incident list; California Department of Fish and Wildlife shark incident database

03Why are there so many sharks in Cape Cod?Partly answeredGray seals came back, and white sharks returned with them. Hunted almost to nothing until bounties ended in the 1960s, gray seals now breed at 9 sites in US waters, up from 1 in 1988, with about 6,700 pups born in 2021. Researchers identified 393 different white sharks off Cape Cod in 2015–2018 and estimate that about 800 visited. Whether more seals mean more bites is an open question.

The evidence, on Research:

  • C31

NOAA gray seal stock assessment (2024); Winton, Fay and Skomal 2023, Marine Ecology Progress Series

To answer this: Cape Cod white shark detections by year. Atlantic White Shark Conservancy (logbook); Massachusetts Division of Marine Fisheries. Ask.

Why it leads isn't something public data can answer yet.
